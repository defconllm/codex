A port that turns ships away starves, and a port that waves them in catches whatever they carry. Ragusa lived on its harbour and could afford neither, so in 1377 it wrote down a third way: a ship from a sick coast came as far as an islet off the city and no further, and waited there thirty days before anyone in the town would touch it. Dr. Allcome keeps the islet. He says yes to every sail in the harbour mouth, the same afternoon, and means it. The yes is to a berth, not to the city.

Thirty days, for a ship that was never sick.

Every merchant in every port said it, and about his own ship he was usually right. About the thirty days he was wrong. At anchor a sick ship and a well one look the same, to the harbour and to themselves, and the days were how anyone found out. The well ship waits so that the sick one cannot say it is well and be believed. A fast lane for whatever looks healthy is a fast lane for whatever has learned to look healthy.

He watches longer for

  • A history only the patient can vouch for.
  • A parcel sealed somewhere he did not see it sealed.
  • A fever that started after the ship tied up.

He does not hurry for

  • Rank. The governor's galley counts its days like a fishing boat.
  • A clean bill from the port it sailed from. That doctor never saw what came aboard after.
  • A full harbour. Crowding opens another court; it never shortens the count.

At the gangway

Never why you came. Everyone may come. He asks three things, and all three go on your chart.

Where have you been?
What did you take on?
When did it start?

The first is the port's question, and the oldest. A ship from a clean coast and a ship from a sick one look the same at anchor, so the coast goes on the chart before the ship does, and he takes it from the log rather than from the captain. The second finds most of what he finds. It asks not what you are but what you took aboard on the way: water from a bad well, a passenger at night, a bale somebody asked you to carry. A sound hull that took on something bad is sick, and it will tell you in all honesty that it feels fine. The third is the physician's question. Illness has an onset, an onset has a time, and the time sits next to something: the letter that came aboard at Zante, the cask filled in the dark. Find the when, and the what is usually lying beside it.

The five orders

What the lazaretto does with everything that comes ashore, before the city puts a hand on it.

Separate
Each ship to its own berth, each crew to its own court. Nobody shares a room, a cup or a well with anyone who came in on another hull, and two ships that arrived together are kept as strangers.
Air
The bales are opened in the yard and aired, by porters who are shut in with them and watched as closely as the crew. Whatever the cargo does, it does inside the wall.
Smoke
Letters are slit and held in smoke; coins and metal go through a bowl of vinegar. The message gets through, and so does the money. Nothing else does.
Count
Thirty days in the ledger, counted from the last fever in that court and not from the day the ship came in. A clock that does not restart on a symptom is not counting anything.
Clear
Free pratique, written and signed: this ship, this day, by the one who watched it. It does not pass to the boat that ties up alongside.

Ragusa called its month a trentino. The ports that copied it made it forty days, and the forty gave the practice its name. Nothing in the list is about keeping anyone out. It is about the city never meeting a ship it has not watched for a month, and a ship never meeting another ship at all. The thirty days are not a wall. They are how long a fever takes to show itself to someone who is looking.

Then he walks back down the quay, because there is another sail in the harbour mouth, and he has never once sent one away.
